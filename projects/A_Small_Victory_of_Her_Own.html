For Chen Lan, a village dance troupe becomes a place to be more than a wife, mother and grandmother.

01

Dancers in a mountain village

At six in the evening, before the sky has quite darkened, women begin arriving in a small courtyard. They belong to the village dance troupe. With rehearsal still a little way off, they sit on a bench and talk.

“Have you practised that move we learned last time?”
“Not yet! There’s been so much to do at home. I was hoping to learn it properly today.”
“We’d better get moving. It’s a county competition!”
“All right, all right!”

Laughter and familiar local voices fill a courtyard that has been quiet all day. Chen Lan rides in shortly before rehearsal begins. Some of the women help unload her props and ask whether her husband has been arguing with her again.

“Let him be. I don’t know why a grown man has so much to say every day,” she replies, waving them towards their positions.

They pick up their props and form their lines. Once the music begins, smiles spread across their faces, as unselfconscious as the joy of a New Year celebration. The sinking sun turns the pale daylight orange. A small speaker’s slightly mechanical backing track mixes with the beat of their drums. Red ribbons swing at their waists; wooden beaters strike; sweat catches the light. For a moment, the courtyard feels like both an escape and a grand dance.

02

A troupe built in the gaps

Born in 1962, Chen grew up in Huaihua, Hunan. During nearly three decades away from home, she worked across much of southern China. In 2014, after spending much of her working life in Shenzhen, she returned to Chenxi County to care for her grandson and granddaughter. She was 52.

Perhaps because she had spent so long unable to raise her own children in person, she was especially attentive to the grandchildren’s upbringing. She combined childcare with casual work in the county. Back in familiar surroundings and an established social circle, she seemed suddenly to discover an interest of her own.

A few close women friends first invited her to sing at their homes. Before long, they were arranging time to dance. Between 2014 and 2015, Chen and women like her had established a form of recreation that belonged to them. After dinner, they came from different corners of the village to a courtyard they had spent a long time finding. They could briefly step outside the family and spend time with one another.

At first, dancing was something Chen felt she had stolen time for. From 2014 to 2016 it remained an intermittent diversion. A new house required decisions about design, labour and materials. The grandchildren were small, and their parents could not take time away to look after them. Chen also cleaned poultry to earn money for household groceries. On top of everything, problems with her health brought a doctor’s warning against overwork.

When fellow dancers called, she often declined on health grounds. “But it wasn’t really much to do with resting,” she says with a smile. Even without the physical problems, there were always things that seemed more important. She knew she was expected to be a good wife, mother and grandmother.

In 2017, the demands that had competed with dancing began to ease. The house was finished. The grandchildren were older. The pain in her back and legs came less often. Chen could give more time to rehearsal. By then, the group was becoming more than an informal gathering.

“We can represent the village now!” The women began entering competitions and performing in the county town. Standards rose, and rehearsals took longer. They bought dedicated costumes and props. Wearing them carried a new sense of occasion: “Once you put those clothes on, your whole spirit feels different.”

Their time was fragmented. Sound equipment was scarce. Their rehearsal space was modest. They practised in the courtyard because there was no studio and borrowed speakers from households in turn. In the spaces left between work and family, they had built a dance troupe.

03

The cost at home

The dancers welcomed the troupe’s progress. In Chen’s household, it brought conflict. Her husband strongly opposed the rehearsals and performances. He did not understand why she needed to squeeze dance into days already filled with cooking, laundry, shopping and childcare. The money spent on costumes angered him: “What a waste!”

A man long accustomed to authority in the household was discovering that his wife had a part of her emotional life beyond him. Her dancing separated time for herself from family members and domestic tasks. At first, his refusal to understand caused her considerable distress. “We argued about it all the time at the beginning. Whenever I went dancing, we argued.” Eventually she began accepting that his support might never come: “He just doesn’t understand. I can’t force him to support me.”

During the 2019 Spring Festival, the troupe performed in the city, representing the county for the first time. Afterwards, the women went walking in the hills. Under a tree near a cliff, they laughed freely and filmed short videos. “We only had less than half a day to enjoy ourselves,” Chen remembers. “But we were so happy.”

It was dark by the time she returned home. The argument that followed was the fiercest since she had taken up dancing. Her husband accused her of letting it make her restless and unruly.

“We went dancing and walking. It’s not as though we were doing something shameful. What’s so unruly about that?” she replied. She drank a large mouthful of cold water, gathered her things and headed upstairs. “I’m exhausted. I can’t be bothered arguing.”

He raised his voice, complaining that shopping and spending money must indeed be tiring, though not as tiring as his work. He had checked the bankbook that day. There was little money, he said, and it was being spent too quickly.

Chen objected to the suggestion that he alone earned the household’s income. “I’m not spending your money. Don’t I earn something from my own work?” She had not abandoned the house: who cooked his meals and washed his clothes? She pointed out that performances also brought a subsidy. Her last words lingered beneath the empty stairwell. Downstairs, the voice grew quieter, but the muttering continued.

The accusation left her hurt and bewildered. She liked dancing. Whom was it harming? Her interest had exposed an imbalance in a family arrangement that had once appeared ordinary. That night, she was unsure why being a capable wife and grandmother should conflict with having an interest of her own. She was certain, however, that she would not give it up.

04

A small victory of her own

After the argument, Chen and her husband stopped speaking. Yet they also reached a limited accommodation over dancing. He continued to oppose it, but no longer took practical steps to stop her or openly expressed his displeasure. Chen stopped struggling to win his understanding. She accepted that he might not provide it.

For more than two weeks, they lived beneath the same roof and ate together without conversation. She disliked his look of disapproval. But she no longer believed that keeping the peace required her always to yield.

The quarrel became a small inward victory. Chen had found a balance between her family roles and a life of her own. More importantly, she would no longer exchange all of herself for the appearance of family harmony. What she could not give up was not simply dancing. It was herself.

05

After rehearsal

Rehearsal ends. The women say goodbye at the courtyard gate and follow different paths home. At the end of the winding lane, Chen’s new house glows with a warm yellow light. The sound of wings draws her gaze upwards.

Pigeons have flown from the loft beside the slope. They scatter in different directions, like small clouds. Perhaps they will return to their enclosure at dusk. For now, they are flying through a patch of sky that belongs to them.

About the reporting

Reporting focus

The profile follows a village dance troupe from courtyard rehearsals to the conflict over one woman’s time at home. Through Chen Lan’s account, it examines how family care, paid work and personal interests compete for space in everyday life.

Chen Lan is a pseudonym; the report describes her age at the time of reporting.

Supporting reporting and questionnaire appendix

Reporting beyond the main profile

The submission also includes field observations of women dancing at Hangzhou’s Tianjie plaza. It contrasts women whose adult children do not yet have children with grandparents who often watch from the edge while caring for grandchildren. The women describe distinctive personal interests: Achun arrives with a Chihuahua and fashionable curls; Meimei leads the group with carefully applied makeup; Shuangyue has danced for five or six years while pursuing weight loss. Chunmei, often occupied with childcare, recalls her own dancing and hopes to find time to join again. Meimei, Achun and Shuangyue are identified as pseudonyms.

Those without grandchildren say they expect to help with childcare in the future. The accompanying reflection considers the tension between family responsibility and personal autonomy, and argues for room to pursue emotional and social life alongside care. It discusses changing family values as a context for that tension, rather than as proof of a single pattern in all Chinese families.

The questionnaire appendix

As of the morning of 8 November 2023, the group reports 130 completed questionnaires, focused on adults aged 30–60. Of these, 119 respondents said they had interests or social circles relatively independent of the family. The questionnaire asked about personal interests, conflict with family life, choices when conflict occurred, regret, intentions to develop a personal life and perceived sources of conflict.

The detailed appendix reports 78.15% experiencing conflict among the 119 respondents with independent interests. It reports 93.62% choosing family first among people experiencing conflict, and 52.13% expressing some regret. The displayed charts split regret into 48.94% “some regret” and 3.19% “considerable regret”.

The text contains inconsistent summaries, including a separate claim of over 95% regret that conflicts with the detailed appendix. Raw responses and a clear denominator reconciliation are not supplied. These are reported figures from a student questionnaire, not population estimates; this page does not build new statistical conclusions from them.

Research rationale and interpretation

The group uses hobbies and independent social networks as an indicator of identity outside family roles. Its hypothesis is that participants increasingly encounter values of autonomy and self-realisation while often continuing to prioritise family obligations, leaving a tension between compromise and a wish for independence. The appendix interprets responsibility towards partners and children as major pressures. It concludes that making room for both care and personal development remains an unfinished task.
