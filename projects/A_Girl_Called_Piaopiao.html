A narrative of leaving home, a final visit to a father, and an attempt to begin again.

01

A child, and a departure

The child was sent at birth to Piaopiao’s adoptive parents: a rural couple with little formal education, unsure how to raise him and afraid that another child might follow the same difficult path. Their house was an unremarkable one among a row of village homes, with a worn wooden door, weathered windows, bare concrete floors and patchy white walls.

When Piaopiao’s adoptive father was alive, the family kept two cattle. The older cow moved slowly, a rough rope darkened by earth, dust and sweat around its neck. A calf followed with a rusty bell and no rope. The draft lingers over the pair as an image of labour and dependence.

Until primary school, Piaopiao’s son stayed with his grandparents. A businessperson from Rui’an later offered financial support through university, giving him a chance to continue his education. The draft describes good school results despite his having missed kindergarten and studying alone in the city.

Piaopiao had left school before he was born, at around 14 or 15. She left home with little luggage or money. How she supported herself at that point remains unclear in the manuscript.

02

Living under other people’s gaze

In the village, neighbours discussed one another while washing clothes, resting after housework or sitting around the dinner table. Rumours about how Piaopiao was supporting herself in another village spread quickly. The draft describes this social scrutiny but supplies no independent corroboration for the rumours.

She appeared to dismiss what others said: their words would not take anything from her. Yet the manuscript also interprets the possibility of pain and self-doubt beneath that defiance. These passages belong to the writer’s narration rather than recorded quotations from Piaopiao.

She moved between places in and around Rui’an and Wenzhou, rarely remaining in one for long. The draft links this unsettled life to substance use and later imprisonment. It does not specify the charge, conviction date or period of custody.

03

A visit home

Prison gave her a fixed routine, clothing and regular meals. The manuscript describes her beginning to think about her birth parents, sisters, adoptive parents, her child and the years she had spent moving from place to place.

Then her adoptive father became seriously ill. The family sought help first locally, then in Rui’an and Wenzhou. Eventually he was taken home. After applications and approvals, Piaopiao was permitted a visit under police escort. The draft describes four officers accompanying her in a police car.

Neighbours gathered at a distance where they could see the house. Inside, the father she remembered as solid and strong lay on a temporary bed. She knelt beside him. Her adoptive mother was nearby; her son stood uncertainly to one side.

“Dad!” she cried. Then: “I was wrong, Dad!” She wanted to communicate love, gratitude and regret in the brief time available. The narrative describes her father trying to respond and Piaopiao struggling to find words. It interprets the exchange as one of love and forgiveness.

Later, she could not clearly recall how the visit ended or how she was taken away. What remained with her was the expression in her father’s eyes.

“Dad! … I was wrong, Dad!”

“爸！……我错了啊！爸！”

Words attributed to Piaopiao in the supplied draft
04

A life with more weight

After release, Piaopiao was still young. The draft returns to its recurring contrast between physical lightness and the growing weight of experience. It describes her recognising that harm to herself could also hurt the people who loved her.

Her hope remained simple: to live, and to live well. The manuscript closes with Piaopiao doing casual work in Wenzhou, supporting herself through her own effort and seeking a more lasting way of living.

The ending rests on a modest change rather than a sweeping resolution: she is working, and she is trying to continue.

About the reporting

Reading this account

The supplied manuscript opens with incomplete lines and contains no clear date, interview record or documentation of the criminal proceedings. This English reading adaptation begins with the first coherent section and attributes uncertain or interpretive material to the draft. It does not add missing scenes or present rumours as established facts. The Chinese source remains available in full.
